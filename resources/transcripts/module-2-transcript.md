# Module 2: What Generative AI Is and Is Not – Transcript

[SLIDE 1: Title – HOOK]

**HOOK**

Ask an AI tool for five academic references and it will give you five. Some of them will not exist.

[SLIDE 2: The puzzle – INTRIGUE]

**INTRIGUE**

And you will not be able to tell which ones by looking. So where does that leave a tool that millions of students and staff now use daily? Is it useless? Far from it. But there is a clear line between what it does well and what it should never be trusted to do, and most mistakes happen when people cross it without noticing. In the next few minutes, I will show you where that line sits.

[SLIDE 3: A useful tool – DELIVERY]

**DELIVERY**

Let's start with what generative AI is good for. It is genuinely useful for shaping, rearranging and exploring language and ideas that you already have in front of you.

It can help you brainstorm angles for an essay or a project. It can turn a messy page of notes into a tidy outline. It can explain a difficult passage in plainer words, translate a paragraph, suggest a clearer structure, or play the part of a sceptical reader and tell you where your argument is hard to follow. For staff, it can draft a routine email, or suggest different ways to explain a tricky concept to a first-year class.

What these uses share is that you supply the substance, or you can easily judge the result. The tool is a sparring partner and a fast first-drafter. It is not the source of the truth.

[SLIDE 4: Not a search engine – DELIVERY]

Now the other side. The most common mistake is to treat a generative AI tool like a search engine.

A search engine finds existing pages and points you to them. You can click through, see who wrote the page, and judge whether to trust it. A generative AI tool produces new text that resembles what a good answer would look like. Sometimes it is accurate. Sometimes it is a smooth blend of fact and fiction, and nothing on the screen tells you which part is which.

Some tools now search the web and summarise what they find, which is a real improvement. But the summary is still generated text, and it can misreport the pages it cites. The sensible habit is to follow the links and read the sources yourself.

[SLIDE 5: Not a library – DELIVERY]

The risk is sharpest with references. A tool may give you a tidy list with authors, titles, journals and page numbers. Some will be real. Some will be invented, with plausible authors and plausible titles that no library holds. A fake reference looks just like a real one, because it was built from the same patterns.

A library catalogue or a published journal is a different kind of thing. Someone chose to put each item there, and you can check it. That is the standard of evidence a university expects.

[SLIDE 6: Not an author or an authority – DELIVERY]

There is a third thing the tool is not: an author. It cannot hold an opinion, stand behind a claim, or be questioned about how it reached a conclusion. It has no reputation to protect and nothing to lose if it is wrong.

That is why responsibility cannot be passed to it. If you put something forward under your name, you are answerable for every claim in it. "The AI told me" is no better a defence than "a stranger in the pub told me".

Here is a simple test to use before any task: "Am I asking the tool to help me think, or to tell me what is true?" The first is a good use. The second needs a trustworthy source alongside it.
