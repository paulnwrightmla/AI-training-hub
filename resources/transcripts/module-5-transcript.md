# Module 5: Good, Ethical and Safe AI Use – Transcript

[SLIDE 1: Title – HOOK]

**HOOK**

Anything you paste into a public AI tool has left your control. You cannot reliably get it back.

[SLIDE 2: The puzzle – INTRIGUE]

**INTRIGUE**

So how do you get the benefits of these tools without putting other people's information, or your own reputation, at risk? It comes down to a few simple guardrails, and a seven-step routine that researchers in higher education have developed to keep people in charge. By the end of this film, you will have both.

[SLIDE 3: Mind what you share – DELIVERY]

**DELIVERY**

Let's start with privacy, because that is where harm is most easily done without anyone noticing.

When you type or paste something into a public AI tool, you are sending it to a company's computers. Depending on the service and your settings, it may be stored, reviewed by people, or used to improve future versions.

So do not put in personal or confidential information. That includes student records and marks, other people's unpublished work, interview transcripts, health or personal details, and unpublished research data. In the United Kingdom, personal data is protected by data protection law, and your university will have its own policy. If in doubt, ask before you paste.

[SLIDE 4: Anonymise, or leave it out – DELIVERY]

Sometimes you have good reason to ask a tool for help with material that contains personal details. First ask whether you need to share it at all. If you do, remove names, places, dates and anything that could identify someone, including combinations of details that point to one person. Do this before you paste, not afterwards. Better still, check whether your university provides an approved tool with stronger protections, and use that.

[SLIDE 5: Credit and over-trust – DELIVERY]

Two further guardrails. First, credit. Other people's work sits behind what these tools produce, and they do not tell you where ideas came from. When you rely on someone's ideas, find and cite the real source, and remember that the final work, and any mistakes in it, are yours.

Second, over-trust. Humans tend to trust a confident machine, especially when busy, and over time we check less and less. This is known as automation bias. The antidote is a deliberate routine.

[SLIDE 6: A routine you can follow – DELIVERY]

That routine is called TRACE-AI. Seven steps.

Target: decide what you are trying to do, and whether AI is appropriate. Request: ask clearly, with context. Audit: read the output critically, as we practised in Module 3. Corroborate: check important claims against reliable sources. Elaborate: do the real thinking yourself, in your own words. Act: make your own decision and be open about how you used AI. Inspect: reflect on what you learned and what you are still unsure about.

You will not need all seven steps for every small task. For work that matters, they keep you in charge.

[SLIDE 7: Declare what you did – DELIVERY]

Being open deserves a closer look. A short declaration of AI use builds trust, and it need only say four things: which tool you used, what for, how you checked the output, and what you contributed yourself. If your university has a standard form, use that.
