# Module 4: The AI-Rich Environment – Transcript

[SLIDE 1: Title – HOOK]

**HOOK**

Software that claims to catch AI-written essays has wrongly flagged work by real students. And it has done so most often for students writing in their second language.

[SLIDE 2: The puzzle – INTRIGUE]

**INTRIGUE**

So if we cannot reliably catch AI use, and we cannot realistically avoid AI altogether, what are universities supposed to do? There is an answer that is gaining ground, and it asks for something different from everyone involved, staff and students alike. Let me show you.

[SLIDE 3: AI is everywhere – DELIVERY]

**DELIVERY**

First, the setting. AI is no longer something you visit on a special website. It is built into word processors, email, search tools and learning platforms. This is what we call an AI-rich environment.

In such an environment, "Did you use AI?" quickly becomes unhelpful. A spelling suggestion uses AI. So does a tool that summarises your emails. A better question is: how, and why, was it used? Was it on purpose, for a clear reason, with the person staying in charge? Or out of habit, to dodge the thinking the task was meant to develop?

[SLIDE 4: Why detection is not the answer – DELIVERY]

When AI tools first spread, many universities hoped for a technical fix: software that could tell whether a text was written by a person or a machine. That hope has not been borne out.

Detectors give probabilities, not proof. They miss AI text that has been lightly edited, and they wrongly flag human writing. Research from Stanford University found that some detectors flagged a large proportion of essays by people whose first language is not English, simply because their careful, formal phrasing resembled what the detector expected from a machine.

Think what that means: an accusation, with real consequences, based on a tool that is least reliable for some of our own international and multilingual students. That is why many universities now rely on clearer rules and better-designed tasks instead.

[SLIDE 5: The four settings – DELIVERY]

Clear rules usually work like a dial, set task by task. Your own institution's wording will differ, so always check its policy, but the idea is similar.

At one end, AI is not permitted, because the point is to show what you can do unaided, as in a closed-book exam or a live discussion. Next is minimal use: spelling, grammar and formatting help, but no generated content. Then permitted use: AI can help with ideas, structure or feedback, but the analysis and writing are your own, and you say how you used it. Finally, integrated use: the task asks you to work with AI and analyse what it does.

None of these settings is better than another. Each fits a different learning purpose.

[SLIDE 6: Designing tasks that value thinking – DELIVERY]

For staff, a clear statement is only half the job. A standard "write 1,000 words summarising this topic" brief is easy to complete with one prompt. Tasks that ask more of the person are harder to hand over: local or personal examples, a short conversation to explain decisions, notes on how the work developed, or evaluating AI output rather than simply producing text.

For students, the matching habit is honesty. Say what you used, what for, and what you checked. Being open protects you far better than trying to hide it.
