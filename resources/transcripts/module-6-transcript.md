# Module 6: Intentional Non-Use – Transcript

[SLIDE 1: Title – HOOK]

**HOOK**

What if the most advanced AI skill is choosing not to use it?

[SLIDE 2: The puzzle – INTRIGUE]

**INTRIGUE**

Over the past five films, we have learned how AI works, where it fails, and how to use it responsibly. But there is one more idea, and it may matter most for anyone whose work is learning and thinking. It concerns something that feels like a cost, but turns out to be a benefit. Let me explain.

[SLIDE 3: Effort is part of learning – DELIVERY]

**DELIVERY**

When something is hard, we usually feel it: the long read of a dense article, the struggle to start an argument, the third attempt at a sentence that will not come out right. We are tempted to see that struggle as a cost to be removed.

But in many cases the struggle is where the learning happens. Reading closely, wrestling with competing evidence, and finding your own words for an idea are the activities that build understanding and memory. They are not obstacles on the way to the learning. They are the learning.

[SLIDE 4: Handing over the effort – DELIVERY]

Psychologists use the term cognitive offloading for passing mental work to an outside aid. We do it all the time, and often sensibly. A shopping list or a calendar reminder frees up your mind for other things.

But there is a paradox. Offloading helps when the freed-up effort is put to better use. If a tool does the very thinking the task was meant to develop, you may finish faster while learning less. Early research suggests that heavy, uncritical reliance on AI tools is associated with weaker recall and less independent critical thinking. This is a developing area, so we should hold the findings with humility. But the logic is familiar from experience: think how a satnav can make us worse at knowing our own town.

[SLIDE 5: Two kinds of task – DELIVERY]

A helpful distinction is between tasks where the output is what matters, and tasks where the process is what matters.

If you need a tidy meeting agenda, the output is the point, and using a tool may be perfectly sensible. If you are a student learning to build an argument, or a lecturer working out how to explain an idea, the process is the point. The thinking is the product. Handing that over is like paying someone else to go to the gym for you.

[SLIDE 6: Times to choose not to – DELIVERY]

So when might you choose not to use AI?

When you are learning something for the first time and need to build a foundation of your own. When you are forming an original idea or position, which should begin in your own head. When your voice and experience are the whole point, such as a personal statement or a reflection on your own practice. When the task is meant to assess what you can do unaided. And when someone needs real human attention, such as sensitive feedback to a student, or support for a struggling colleague.

[SLIDE 7: A choice, not a rule – DELIVERY]

This is not an argument against AI. It is an argument for choice. Using the tools well and sometimes leaving them alone are two halves of the same skill. A useful question is: "If this tool vanished tomorrow, would I still be able to do this?" If the answer is no, and you need to be able to, that is the signal to practise unaided.

For staff, the same applies to course design: build in moments where students think, write and talk without AI, and explain why. When people understand the reason, they value those moments.

[SLIDE 8: Your next step – EXIT]

**EXIT**

For your final exercise, write a short piece about your own academic or professional journey with no tools at all, then compare it with an AI version of the same piece. Notice which one is yours. Then take a moment to decide one thing you will deliberately keep doing without AI. Thank you for taking part in this series.
